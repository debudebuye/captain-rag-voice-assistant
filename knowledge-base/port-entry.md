# Port Entry (SAMPLE — demo knowledge base)

> This document is sample content created for a demo knowledge base. It is not
> official maritime guidance and must not be used for real ship operations.

## Pilot and VTS Requirements

Any vessel over 200 meters in length, vessels carrying dangerous cargo, and vessels with
engine or steering defects are required to take a harbour pilot when entering a port where
compulsory pilotage applies. The pilot boarding point is announced on the VTS channel.
While the pilot is on board, the Master remains in command of the vessel, but the pilot
advises on navigation. The pilot's instructions are to be followed unless the Master
decides that they would endanger the vessel. Entry is refused when draught exceeds the
maximum draught declared for the berth or approach channel.

## Arrival Preparation

At four hours before arrival, the engines must be tested on ahead and astern, along with
the steering gear, whistles, navigational lights, and the telegraph. One hour before
arrival, the deck crew stands by at the fore and aft stations with heaving lines, and the
accommodation ladder is swung out to the port side after the vessel has secured permission.
The officer of the watch reports anchoring information with the ship's GPS position, the
depth of water, and the number of shackles of cable paid out.

## Docking and Mooring

When berthing, the Master brings the vessel alongside slowly and uses a combination of the
main engine and bow thruster to control the approach angle. The spring lines are made fast
first, followed by the head lines and the stern lines. Mooring lines are doubled up if the
port expects strong weather or high tidal range. After mooring, the gangway is rigged, the
gangway net is placed beneath it, and a gated or manned gangway watch is posted. The
G flag (Golf) is hoisted when the vessel requires a pilot.

## Anchoring Procedure

Before anchoring, the Master selects an anchorage clear of traffic lanes and cables, with
good holding ground and a depth of about three times the draught plus the expected tide.
The anchor is walked back under power on the windlass and the cable is marked with chain
paint at every shackle. On reaching a good hold, the cable is allowed to veer out to the
designed scope, usually five to seven times the water depth in moderate weather. The
vessel is then brought up and the position monitored on the GPS and by two radar ranges to
confirm the anchor is holding. Vessels anchoring near a cable must gain permission from
the cable owner.

## Anchorage in Port Approaches

Tankers and vessels carrying dangerous goods anchor at designated anchorage positions and
report anchoring information. Vessels not permitted to anchor include those within 500
meters of a submarine cable or pipeline and those inside a traffic lane. In strong wind
and tidal conditions, a second anchor is dropped on the same scope length as the first.
When the anchor is weighed, the chain is examined for damage and the anchor is secured for
the sea with a stopper.

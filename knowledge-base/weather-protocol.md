# Weather Protocol (SAMPLE — demo knowledge base)

> This document is sample content created for a demo knowledge base. It is not
> official maritime guidance and must not be used for real ship operations.

## Heavy Weather Preparation

When heavy weather is expected, the Master must be informed immediately. All hatches and
watertight doors must be closed and secured. Deck cargo must be checked and re-lashed if
necessary. Fuel and water tanks should be topped up. A general inspection of all life-saving
equipment, including lifeboats, must be carried out. The crew must reduce all loose gear on
the poop deck and in the holds. Engineering should verify that the ballast system is ready
and that bilge levels are normal.

## Weather Warning Levels

Beaufort scale references guide decisions on board: at Force 6 (strong breeze, 25 knots),
additional mooring lines may be required. At Force 8 (gale, 40 knots), all personnel must
remain below deck unless essential on-deck duties are required and the Master has
authorised it. At Force 10 (storm, 55 knots), the ship must alter course and speed to
minimise rolling and slamming, typically by putting the vessel's head 30 to 50 degrees off
the wind. At Force 12 (hurricane, 64 knots and above), the ship's structure may not be
designed to withstand the loads and the Master must seek shelter or deviate well outside
the weather system.

## Following Seas and Rogue Waves

Vessels with a deep draft and a flat bottom are particularly susceptible to broaching when
running before a following sea. The critical situation is when the wave period matches the
ship's natural rolling period. At this point, the Master should reduce speed by at least
20 percent to reduce the relative speed of the wave and regain control. For an encounter
with an exceptionally large wave, the Master must order all watertight doors closed and
shift engine power to full ahead to ride up onto the wave rather than be driven over.

## Wind and Current Effects on Course Keeping

Wind has a greater influence on the ship's heading than current because of the large
windage area of the superstructure and containers on deck. When the wind is coming from
the side, the ship tends to drift downwind and weather helm may be required to keep the
ship on course. Current has a greater effect on the ship's position than on the heading.
For a course made good, the heading must be corrected for the leeway and set to the
current. The ship's position after an hour of steaming is calculated from the total run
and the current vector using the DR position, and the error is checked against GPS.

## Weather Routeing and Forecasts

Weather routeing is used when a voyage crosses an ocean. The routeing service recommends a
route that minimises heavy weather exposure against total distance. Each day, the Master
receives two weather forecasts by INMARSAT email: the surface analysis and the 48-hour
forecast. High pressure systems bring settled weather and lighter winds, while low pressure
systems bring stormy weather and stronger winds, with wind blowing counter-clockwise
around the low in the northern hemisphere. The ship should avoid the semicircle of a
tropical storm and divert course well clear of the storm's path.
